Guarded Confirm

Fitts' Law for irreversible actions

Scenario

User & context. Someone sending a peer-to-peer payment on a large phone — splitting a bill, paying rent, sending money home. Standing on a train or walking out of a restaurant: one hand occupied, one thumb on the glass, attention arriving in two-second glances.

The problem. The last tap in the flow is irreversible. It must be fast, because it is the 99% case and fiddly confirmations are the top complaint in these apps. It must also be hard to hit by accident, because it sits beside Cancel, and a slip in that direction throws away a half-finished transfer while a slip the other way sends money that cannot be recalled.

What designers actually argue about. Where the confirm button goes (top nav bar, docked under the keypad, or down at the bottom edge behind the home-indicator gutter), how tall it is (Material's 48dp floor, or below it as dense banking layouts ship, or comfortably above), and how much empty space guards it from its neighbour — a number that is almost always eyeballed.

Innovation: pricing the guard gap

What this study adds to a textbook Fitts task.

  • Fitts' Law on its own always concludes bigger, closer, no gap — because movement time is its only currency. For an irreversible action that conclusion is wrong.
  • So this instrument treats Confirm and its neighbour as a target pair and logs two outcomes: movement time, and what the tap actually landed on.
  • It then reports an expected cost per payment: E[cost] = MT + p(slip) × recovery cost. Guard spacing buys down p(slip) and sells off MT, and this puts both on the same axis so a layout can be chosen rather than argued over. You set the recovery cost below.
  • Targets are rectangles approached at an angle, so width is the target's projected extent along the movement axis (W′ = w·|uₓ| + h·|u_y|), not its nominal width.
